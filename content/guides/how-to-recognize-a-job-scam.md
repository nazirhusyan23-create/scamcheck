Job scams have grown significantly alongside the rise of remote work. Many follow a
recognizable script — here's what to watch for.

## Pay that's too good for the effort described

Offers of high daily or hourly pay for very simple tasks — "just reply to messages," "easy data
entry," "no experience needed" — are a classic lure. Real employers rarely need to overpay for
minimal-skill work.

## Being asked to buy equipment first

One of the most well-documented job scam patterns: you're told you need to purchase a laptop or
"starter kit" from a specific supplier before you can begin. This is designed purely to extract
a payment from you — real employers provide or reimburse necessary equipment, they don't require
you to buy it upfront from a seller they specify.

## Requests for bank details too early

Be cautious if you're asked for full bank account information before any real interview or offer
letter has been provided.

## No real interview process

Being "hired" instantly with no interview, or an interview conducted entirely through chat
messages with no video or phone call, is common in fake job schemes.

## Recruitment only through Telegram or WhatsApp

Legitimate hiring processes usually involve a company email address and, often, a real careers
page. Be cautious if all communication happens exclusively through personal messaging apps with
no way to verify the company.

## Requests for payment in gift cards or cryptocurrency

Being asked to pay "training," "registration," or "software license" fees via gift card or
cryptocurrency is a strong scam indicator — these payment methods are difficult to trace or
reverse.

## A note on grammar

Poor grammar or spelling in a job posting is sometimes cited as a scam indicator, but treat it as
a minor signal at best. Many legitimate postings have typos, and well-written scams are common
too. Don't rely on grammar alone.

## What to do

- Research the company independently — check their official website and LinkedIn presence.
